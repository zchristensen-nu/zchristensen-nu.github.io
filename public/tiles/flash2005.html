Building a comment overlay for prototypes

SEP 14, 2026 · 6 MIN READtoolingprototypingnode

I review a lot of local prototypes: mostly my own, and more and more often ones an agent built for me. The slow part was never the review. It was the ritual around it: take a screenshot, crop it, paste it into a chat, then explain which button I meant. So I built claude-feedback, a small proxy that puts a comment layer on top of anything running on localhost.

One port in front of every port

The proxy listens on 4599 and treats the first path segment as the target port, so localhost:4599/3000/about fetches localhost:3000/about. Everything passes through untouched, including assets, websockets and hot reload. The one exception is HTML: those responses get a single script tag injected just before the closing body tag.

proxy.jsJAVASCRIPT
// route /<port>/<path> to the real dev serverconst [, port, ...rest] = req.url.split('/');proxy.web(req, res, { target: `http://localhost:${port}` });// HTML only: inject the overlay before </body>html = html.replace('</body>', OVERLAY + '</body>');

Comments are just JSON

Click anywhere and you get a pin and a text box. Saving writes one record to ~/.claude-feedback/comments.json: the page URL, where the pin sits, the note, a screenshot of what I was looking at, and resolved: false. There's no database and no accounts. A JSON file turned out to be the right interface, because both I and an agent can read it without any glue.

The best review tool is the one that removes the step where you describe what you're pointing at.

— Note to self, taped to the monitor

Closing the loop

Now I leave a handful of pins and say "address my feedback". The agent pulls the unresolved comments for that page, looks at each screenshot, makes the fix, and flips resolved to true. The overlay notices and turns the pin green. It's a small tool, but it changed how fast I can iterate on a UI: I used to get through a few rounds an hour, and now it's a few rounds per coffee.

What I learned shipping a daily news digest agent

AUG 21, 2026 · 8 MIN READagentsautomationwriting

Morning Briefing has gone out every weekday for a few months now. It reads a fixed list of outlets, follows the stories that are still developing, and writes a digest that lands before 7am. Here's what I learned, roughly in the order I learned it.

1. Protocol beats vibes

The first version was told to "find the important news". It found whatever ranked well. The version that works walks an explicit list of sources, in order, every day, and has to account for each one. It's boring, reliable and auditable, which is exactly what you want from something that runs while you're asleep.

2. Running threads are the product

On most days the valuable item isn't a new story. It's the next beat of an old one. Keeping a short list of live threads to check first made the digest read like it was written by someone who had also read yesterday's.

briefing.configYAML
schedule: "30 6 * * 1-5"   # weekdays, 06:30sources:  outlets.txt       # checked in order, every runthreads:  threads.md        # follow these up firststyle:    no-bullets, label-op-eds, max-12-itemsdeliver:  email @ 06:45

3. Write the style guide down

No bullets. No bolded summary lines. Op-eds get labeled as op-eds. Every rule I added came from a morning when the output was technically fine and still wrong. The rules are the taste, written down somewhere a model can read them.

4. Decide what "nothing happened" looks like

Slow days are when it wants to pad. Giving it explicit permission to be short made the whole thing more trustworthy on the days that do matter.

An agent that runs every day is less a program than an editor you have to keep briefing.

— From my notes, week six

Notes on designing for motion

JUN 30, 2026 · 5 MIN READmotiondesigncss

I spent a week rebuilding a Flash-era portfolio in CSS for fun, and it reminded me that the motion vocabulary of 2005 was better than its reputation. Sure, the intros were too long. But the people making those sites thought hard about easing.

Ease out, then overshoot a little

Almost everything on this page uses one curve: a fast start, a slight overshoot, and a settle. In ActionScript it was Back.easeOut. In CSS it's a cubic-bezier with a control point above 1. It makes a panel feel like it has mass without making you wait for it.

motion.cssCSS
:root {  --ease-flash: cubic-bezier(.19, 1.45, .36, 1);  --t-panel: 600ms;}.panel.is-entering {  animation: wipe-in var(--t-panel) var(--ease-flash) both;}

Motion should explain where things went

A wipe from left to right says "next". An iris opening from the card you clicked says "this came from there". If a transition doesn't answer where something came from or where it went, it's decoration, and it should be shorter.

The best part of the Flash era was the craft. The worst part was being held hostage by it.

— Me, after watching a 40-second intro

Always ship the off switch

Every animation here collapses to an instant change under prefers-reduced-motion, and the intro has had a skip button since the first frame. Sound is off until you ask for it. Motion is a thing you offer people, not a thing you do to them.
